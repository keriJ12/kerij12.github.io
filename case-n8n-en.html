Fabric Enquiry Workflow in n8n — AI drafts, rules decide, a person approves

A customer sends a loosely written enquiry. The workflow uses AI to turn it into fields, uses fixed rules to pick the route and list what is still missing, looks up stock and lead time in a fabric list, and emails a review request to the person in charge. Only after that person picks a decision is anything logged. When the customer needs to be asked something, a reply draft is ready, and it is never sent automatically. This is a working prototype on n8n Cloud. All data is fictional.

01Context

Situation

There are two kinds of enquiry and a hundred ways to write them

Customer service at a textile mill receives enquiries every day. Some customers name a model and want more of it. Others only describe the fabric they have in mind: black, linen, for shirts, around 160 g/m². Quantity and delivery date are often missing, or given as “about”. Someone has to read the email, check whether a suitable fabric exists, and decide who handles it next.

This case extends the “customer service prepares the request” step of WEAVE. WEAVE simulates the whole handoff in the browser. Here, the first small part is connected to a real mailbox, a real spreadsheet and a real language model, to see what happens when it actually runs.

02Scope

Scope

What it does, and what it deliberately does not

Does

  • Starts when an email arrives; a second version starts from a paste-in form
  • AI turns the email into fixed fields
  • A rule picks the route: model named, or properties only
  • Looks up the fabric list: stock, minimum order, lead time, discontinued or not
  • Rules list the questions to ask the customer
  • Emails a review request; a person picks one of three decisions
  • Logs every decision
  • Prepares a reply draft when the case is sent back
  • Logs failures and emails an alert

Deliberately does not

  • Let AI pick the route, decide what to ask, or judge whether an order can be taken
  • Send anything to the customer automatically
  • Let AI guess a quantity, date or model that is not in the email
  • Use real company data or real customers
  • Create orders or notify development after approval

03Five stages

Overview

From incoming email to a result in five stages

Stage by stage

Each stage does one thing

  • 1. Intake: picks up only unread emails sent to the enquiry address, keeps five fields (body, sender, subject and two conversation IDs), drops the rest, and marks the email as read so it is not processed twice.
  • 2. Extract: a language model translates the email into fixed fields (customer, model, fibre, colour, quantity, weight, width, use, delivery date). Anything not stated stays empty. Rules, not AI, then list the questions for the customer.
  • 3. Look up fabrics: if a model is named, that row is looked up. If not, fabrics are matched by fibre, colour, weight and width; conditions the customer did not give are skipped. Both routes end in the same data shape.
  • 4. Human review: the person in charge receives an email with the extracted fields, the fabric list result, the open questions and the original email, then picks approve, send back, or hand to development. Nothing is created or sent before this.
  • 5. Log and act: the decision is written to a log. On “send back”, a reply draft appears under the customer's original email. Sending it is up to the person.

04Five decisions

Decision 1 · Where AI sits

AI translates. Rules decide.

At first the AI also decided what to ask the customer. I ran the same set of test emails three times. Each round, one or two came out wrong, and never in the same place: asking to confirm a quantity the customer had stated exactly, treating the customer's question to us as our question to them, mixing up quantity and weight. The longer the prompt got, the more it dropped.

I moved that job to rules. An empty field becomes a question. A number preceded by “around” or “about” becomes a request to confirm. When a model is named, weight and width come from the fabric list, so they are not asked. After that, the same email produced the same questions every time. The AI keeps the one job it does reliably, which is turning a messy email into fields.

Decision 2 · Fixed options

Fibre must be one of five words, or empty

The customer writes “100% linen”; the fabric list says “Linen 100%”. To a computer these are different. So there is an extra fibre field, and the AI may only return one of five fixed words. Matching is then done by rules. When the customer names a fibre outside the list, such as silk, the composition is copied as written and fibre stays empty. Nothing matches, and a person decides.

Decision 3 · Three outcomes

“Nothing found” and “nothing to search with” are different

During testing I typed a string of digits into the form. The review screen listed almost the entire fabric list. The matching rule said “skip conditions the customer did not give”, and this input gave none, so everything passed. That is worse than an empty result, because it looks like a result.

The lookup now has three explicit outcomes: matching fabrics are listed; there were conditions but nothing matched, shown as NO MATCH, which suits handing to development; the email gave nothing to search with, shown as NOT SEARCHED, which suits sending back to the customer.

Decision 4 · No lost emails

When nothing matches, the workflow must not end silently

The first version used a filter node for matching. With a test email asking for red silk, no fabric matched and the workflow simply stopped there: no review screen, no log entry, no notice. Yet “we do not have this fabric” is exactly the case that most needs a person.

The fix was to change the order: collect the whole list into one package first, then pick from inside it. An empty pick is still one package, so the workflow carries on to review. The cost is that the matching rule went from five visible conditions to one expression, which is harder to read. For enquiries, not losing an email matters more than tidiness.

Decision 5 · Where the person sits

Review happens in the mailbox, and replies stop at a draft

The reviewer does not need to log in to n8n. They read the email and decide. A review link cannot be answered twice. On “send back”, the workflow builds a reply from the open questions, places it under the customer's original email with the recipient filled in, and stops at a draft. If the customer gave no name, the greeting falls back to a general form instead of being left blank.

05When it fails

Two layers

Expected problems are handled in the node; unexpected ones go to a separate workflow

  • Node level: spreadsheet nodes retry automatically. A lookup that finds nothing sends an empty result so the workflow continues.
  • Workflow level: a separate error-handling workflow. When any workflow attached to it fails, it writes the workflow name, the failed node, the error message and a link to the execution into an error log, and emails an alert. It is separate because a broken workflow cannot report on itself, and one handler can serve several workflows.
It happened

A real error, found within a minute

During one edit I pasted another node's text into the AI prompt. The person submitting the form only saw “please try again”, with no reason. The execution record showed which node failed and what data was missing. I corrected it, published again, and it recovered. This was not a staged test. It was the first time the error handling was needed.

06Limits

What was checked

Ten kinds of test input, through both versions

A named model, a model that does not exist, a model that is out of stock, properties only, loose conditions with two matches, a match that is discontinued, a fibre the list does not carry, no sender name, an empty email, and random typing. Both versions were run end to end while published, with real emails and real form submissions. This is my own functional testing. I have not measured accuracy over a large volume of emails.

Limits

This is a prototype, some way from production

  • Nothing happens after approval: the three decisions differ only in the log and in whether a draft is prepared. No order is created and nobody is notified of a handover to development.
  • The data is fictional: the fabric list has 12 rows and is read in full each time. Thousands of real rows would need a query instead, or the spreadsheet's read limits would be hit.
  • English and metric only: yards, ounces, or emails in German or Chinese are not handled. The “approximate” check knows only a few English words and needs a unit after the number.
  • One email at a time: if two emails arrive in the same minute, the way later steps fetch data may let them interfere with each other.
  • Emails are marked read before processing: a failure mid-way is not retried automatically; the error alert tells a person to handle it.
  • Real-world email noise is not handled: attachments, quoted forwards, signatures, one email asking about three fabrics.
  • No duplicate check: the same email arriving twice is logged twice.
  • Personal trial account: no permission levels; the mailbox is a separate demo account.
